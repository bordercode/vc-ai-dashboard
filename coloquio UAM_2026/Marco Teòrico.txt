Theoretical frameworks for studying data center locations and their environmental and social implications in Mexico
For an academic paper examining the location of data centers in Mexico and their environmental and social implications, I would recommend combining political ecology, environmental justice, and economic geography. These frameworks address complementary dimensions of the problem: the distribution of environmental costs, the power relations behind infrastructure development, and the economic and territorial factors that influence where data centers are built.
The key is to avoid treating data center locations as purely technical or economic decisions. Their geographical distribution also reflects access to electricity, water, telecommunications, land, investment incentives, and political power. These factors can create uneven environmental and social outcomes across Mexican regions.
1. Political ecology
Main question: How do power relations and economic interests shape the environmental consequences of data center development?
Political ecology examines the relationships between political and economic power, environmental change, and the distribution of natural resources.
Applied to Mexican data centers, this framework would allow you to investigate:
- Water consumption: How data center cooling requirements interact with local water scarcity and competing demands from households, agriculture, and industry.
- Electricity demand: How data center expansion affects local energy systems and the distribution of environmental burdens associated with electricity generation.
- Land use: How infrastructure development changes land use and affects surrounding communities and ecosystems.
- Power asymmetries: Whether technology companies, governments, and local communities have unequal influence over decisions concerning infrastructure development.
For example, you could investigate whether data centers are being developed in regions where water resources are already under pressure, and how government policies and corporate priorities influence these decisions.
Why it is useful for Mexico: Political ecology provides a way to examine the relationship between infrastructure investment, resource governance, and regional inequalities. It is particularly appropriate if your paper seeks to understand not only where data centers are located, but also why those locations are selected and who bears the environmental costs.
A limitation is that political ecology can be broad. You would need to operationalize it through specific concepts, such as resource governance, power asymmetry, and environmental externalities.
2. Environmental justice
Main question: Who benefits from data center development, and who bears its environmental and social costs?
Environmental justice is concerned with the fair distribution of environmental benefits and burdens, the participation of affected communities in decision-making, and the recognition of their needs and rights.
A useful approach is to distinguish three dimensions:
- Distributive justice: Are the environmental burdens of data centers, including water demand, electricity consumption, and pollution associated with energy production, concentrated in particular communities?
- Procedural justice: Do local communities have meaningful opportunities to participate in decisions about infrastructure development?
- Recognitional justice: Are the needs, experiences, and rights of different groups adequately recognized, including those of rural communities and populations experiencing water insecurity?
You could use this framework to compare data center locations with indicators of water stress, socioeconomic vulnerability, access to public services, and local energy infrastructure.
For instance, your research could examine whether the benefits of digital infrastructure and foreign investment are distributed differently from its resource demands and environmental costs.
Why it is useful for Mexico: Environmental justice gives your paper a clear social dimension and provides a structured way to investigate inequality. It is particularly relevant if you want to determine whether the costs and benefits of data center development are distributed equitably.
An important distinction: the presence of a data center in a water-stressed municipality does not, by itself, establish that the facility causes water insecurity or environmental injustice. You would need evidence about actual resource consumption, competing demands, and affected populations.
